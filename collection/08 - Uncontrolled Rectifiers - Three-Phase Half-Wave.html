RECTIFIERS / THREE-PHASE HALF WAVE

Three-phase half-wave uncontrolled rectifier

Three phases take turns. One neutral closes the path. Follow the diode connected to the highest phase voltage. See why the output has three pulses per period, why the neutral carries current, and what inductive and capacitive filters really change.

What to notice now

Selection windows are not always conduction intervals

With positive U–V–W sequence, D1 has the highest phase from 30° to 150°, D2 from 150° to 270°, and D3 from 270° to 390° (across 360°). With an opposing voltage or a capacitor, the selected diode can still be blocked.

Why this circuit needs a neutral

This is a four-wire connection. Each phase current is unidirectional, with a positive mean when power is transferred. Their sum returns through N. Do not apply the zero-neutral-current rule for balanced sinusoidal loads to these rectified currents.

Guided investigations

01 · PREDICT → TEST → EXPLAIN

Predict the conducting phase

Start with ideal R. At 210°, which phase is highest? Predict the signs of io, iU, and iN before loading the test case.

Reveal the explanation

Phase V is highest, so D2 conducts. The output and neutral-return currents are positive, but the phase-U current is zero. A negative phase-U voltage does not reverse current through D1: D1 blocks.

02 · PREDICT → TEST → EXPLAIN

Why 120°, not 180°?

In the ideal R case, phase U is still positive at 151°. Predict whether D1 is still conducting. Compare the cursor immediately before and after 150°.

Reveal the explanation

D1 turns off because phase V becomes higher, not because phase U becomes negative. Positive phase voltage alone is not sufficient. With ideal continuous conduction, each diode occupies only its 120° highest-phase window.

03 · PREDICT → TEST → EXPLAIN

Smooth current without smoothing voltage

Prepare the ideal RL case and keep Vm, f, and R fixed. Predict the effect of changing L from 0 to 350 mH on mean current, current ripple, and output voltage.

Reveal the explanation

The inductor stores and releases energy, reducing current ripple. The average current stays Vo,dc/R, because the mean inductor voltage is zero. The three-pulse output voltage remains imposed by the source, and the neutral still carries the output current.

04 · PREDICT → TEST → EXPLAIN

Do three balanced phases cancel?

Use ideal R and look at 90°, 210°, and 330°. Predict the neutral-return current at each angle. Does its mean vanish?

Reveal the explanation

The phase currents are pulses of the same sign. Their shifted sum is the output current, not zero. Each phase has mean Io,dc/3; the neutral has mean Io,dc with the return reference shown. Balanced voltages do not remove this DC component.

05 · PREDICT → TEST → EXPLAIN

Count one diode drop

Prepare ideal R at Vm = 20 V. Predict the mean-voltage change when you enable a 1 V diode drop. Compare the path with a single-phase bridge.

Reveal the explanation

Only one diode conducts in the active source path. In this prepared case, conduction remains continuous, so the mean output falls by 1 V. A four-diode single-phase bridge has two series diode drops in its active path.

06 · PREDICT → TEST → EXPLAIN

Highest phase, but no current?

Prepare an RLE load with E = 280 V. Predict what happens as E rises above the 325 V phase peak. Watch both current and voltage during blocked intervals.

Reveal the explanation

At 280 V the current is discontinuous for the prepared settings. When E reaches the available phase peak minus the diode drop, no positive steady-state current can start. During blocking, the output is E. Inductance can extend an existing pulse, but cannot sustain periodic power transfer when the source can never overcome E.

07 · PREDICT → TEST → EXPLAIN

Smoother voltage, sharper source current

Prepare R‖C. Predict the effect of increasing C on voltage ripple and source-current peaks. Then move just after 90°: can D1 conduct while the capacitor discharges?

Reveal the explanation

Larger C reduces voltage ripple but concentrates replenishment into shorter intervals. Immediately after a peak, iC can be negative while io remains positive: the source and capacitor then supply the resistor together. Turn-off occurs at io = 0, not at iC = 0. The repetition frequency remains 3f for this balanced periodic circuit.

Model and equations

During series-load conduction, the selected phase supplies the resistor, inductor, and opposing DC source of voltage E through one diode. From zero current, turn-on requires the highest phase voltage to exceed E + VF. Inductive current can continue after that inequality stops being true. Set L = 0 for R or RE; E = 0 when the opposing source is disabled.

Ldiodt+Rio+E=vo
vo=max⁡(vU,vV,vW)−VF

For R‖C, a conducting diode imposes vo = max(vU, vV, vW) − VF. It turns off when io reaches zero, not necessarily at the voltage peak. Charging pulses here are idealized: source impedance and startup inrush are not modeled.

io=iR+iC=voR+Cdvodt

With all diodes blocked: io = 0. For a series load, vo = E; for R‖C, the capacitor sets vo and discharges through the resistor.

Conventions, limitations, and references

U, V, W are upright phase terminal names; italic R is resistance. Phase-current subscripts U, V, W identify source phases; subscript R identifies the load resistor. With R‖C, iR = vo/R and the complete rectifier output current is io = iR + iC. The main current cards and current ripple factor refer to the resistor branch; a separate RMS readout includes capacitor current. With a series load, the output and resistor currents coincide. All means and RMS values refer to one complete source period. Output and neutral current are positive toward the load and back to N, respectively. At ideal commutation, the display uses the right-hand limit, with no finite overlap interval.

vU=Vm⁢sin⁡(θ)
vV=Vm⁢sin⁡(θ−2π3)
vW=Vm⁢sin⁡(θ−4π3)
θ=ωt,ω=2πf

The model assumes a balanced sinusoidal voltage source with an accessible neutral and zero source impedance. It excludes startup, source or transformer inductance, overlap, leakage, reverse recovery, switching loss, parasitic capacitance, temperature, transformer losses, and magnetic saturation. The DC in individual phase currents matters for transformer design, which is not modeled. Never use the ideal charging-current peaks or diode stresses as component ratings.

The curves represent periodic steady state, not a transient after each slider change. Piecewise analytical solutions and bracketed switching events are integrated with event-split Gaussian quadrature. Before displaying results, the model checks Kirchhoff laws, the independent inductor and capacitor derivative laws, conducting/blocked diode conditions, and power/periodic-energy balances. Ripple factors are evaluated directly from deviations about the corresponding mean; stored-energy states are checked for continuity at switching.

CSV uses SI base units and a decimal point. The grid is event-enhanced, not uniformly sampled; equal angles with limit_side values left and right describe jumps. output_current_A includes capacitor current; resistor_current_A does not. Technical provenance and parameter metadata precede the waveform table. Existing phase column names retain R, S, T for compatibility; the displayed mapping is R→U, S→V, T→W. Parameter names and waveform values are unchanged. Separate result rows record resistor-current mean and RMS. The existing technical field result_phase_current_nonfundamental_including_dc_ratio is the DC-included source-current THD shown here; its definition and values are unchanged. Both distortion fields are dimensionless ratios, not percentages; both use phase fundamental RMS as denominator.
