Cursor angle and initial phase: different roles

One time coordinate for all voltages

θ=ωt

θ is the electrical angle elapsed since t = 0. Moving the cursor selects another instant on the same waveforms.

An initial phase for each voltage

φU=0°
φV=−120°
φW=120°

The constants φU, φV, and φW are the sine phases at t = 0. They do not change when the cursor moves.

Instantaneous phase: add the two angles

vV=Vm⁢sin⁡(θ+φV)

The sine argument of vV is θ + φV. For vU it equals θ because φU = 0; that does not make every voltage's phase equal to the cursor angle.

These initial phases use the chosen sine convention and time origin. A different time origin would change their values, not their differences; moving the cursor does not change that origin. Arguments written with π use radians; readouts and examples marked ° use degrees.

Example: V when the cursor is at 90°

For a nonzero amplitude, at θ = 90°, φV remains −120°. The instantaneous phase is 90° − 120° = −30° and vV = −Vm/2. The 90° cursor position is not the initial phase of V.

Phase voltages · Fundamentals

Three phases, one frequency

The three voltages are measured relative to the same neutral N. They have the same peak Vm and frequency f, and are separated by 120° (2π/3 rad). The fixed initial phases are φU = 0, φV = −2π/3, and φW = +2π/3; θ is the common time angle.

vU=Vm⁢sin⁡(θ)
vV=Vm⁢sin⁡(θ−2π3)
vW=Vm⁢sin⁡(θ+2π3)
What to notice now

Track the positive maxima: U, then V, then W. The 120° spacing is between matching points of different phases—not between any two neighboring zero crossings.

Why does this happen?
At every instant, vU + vV + vW = 0. The vectors form a balanced set; their vertical projections also sum to zero. This does not mean that the three voltages are individually zero.
vU+vV+vW=0

The equivalent expression vW = Vm sin(θ − 4π/3) is identical to Vm sin(θ + 2π/3): the arguments differ by one complete turn, 2π rad. These are equivalent ways to write the fixed initial phase of W, not different cursor positions.

Line voltages · Potential differences

A line voltage is a difference—not a new source

For vUV, put the positive probe at U and the negative probe at V. Subtract both instantaneous potentials at the same instant. The resulting initial phases are φUV = +π/6, φVW = −π/2, and φWU = +5π/6. They are constant, unlike θ.

vUV=3Vm⁢sin⁡(θ+π6)
vVW=3Vm⁢sin⁡(θ−π2)
vWU=3Vm⁢sin⁡(θ+5π6)
What to notice now

vUV, vVW, and vWU lead vU, vV, and vW, respectively, by 30°. Their peak is √3Vm; the line voltages still differ from one another by 120°.

Derive √3 and the 30° shift

U and V are 120° apart, not 180°. Their positive and negative peaks do not occur together. Subtracting the two rotating vectors gives a vector √3 times as long, rotated 30° ahead of U.

vUV=vU−vV
vUV=Vm[sin⁡(θ)−sin⁡(θ−2π3)]
vUV=2Vm⁢sin⁡(π3)⁢cos⁡(θ−π3)
vUV=3Vm⁢sin⁡(θ+π6)

Reference polarity · Terminal order

Changing the terminal order changes the sign

vUW, vWV, and vVU are the reversed-polarity counterparts of vWU, vVW, and vUV, respectively. The source is unchanged: reversing a measurement pair does not reverse the U–V–W phase sequence. Their fixed initial phases are φUW = −π/6, φWV = +π/2, and φVU = −5π/6.

vUW=3Vm⁢sin⁡(θ−π6)
vWV=3Vm⁢sin⁡(θ+π2)
vVU=3Vm⁢sin⁡(θ−5π6)
What to notice now

For this U–V–W source, vUW, vWV, and vVU lag vU, vW, and vV, respectively, by 30°. Reversing one fixed pair—UV to VU—changes its phase by 180°, not 30°.

Check the three polarity identities
vUW=−vWU
vWV=−vVW
vVU=−vUV

The meter reports the potential of its positive terminal minus that of its negative terminal. Swapping the probes negates every sample. A negated sine wave has the same peak and RMS and is shifted by 180°.

The complete line-voltage set

Six polarities, three distinct phase pairs

Display all six line voltages over two periods. Opposite polarities are exact negatives. The six positive peaks occur at 60° intervals.

Positive-peak order as the cursor θ advances from 0°: WV → UV → UW → VW → VU → WU → WV. These are peak positions, not initial phases or a new phase sequence.

The three distinct phase pairs are UV, VW, and WU; they are not three algebraically independent voltages: vUV + vVW + vWU = 0.

From the overlay to a maximum–minimum envelope

At any instant, the largest positive line voltage is obtained by subtracting the lowest phase potential from the highest. Select “Show maximum–minimum envelope” to make that relationship visible. This optional extension is a mathematical construction, not a simulation of a rectifier power circuit.

vspan=max⁡(vU,vV,vW)−min⁡(vU,vV,vW)

For the balanced source, 1.5Vm ≤ vspan ≤ √3Vm. Two phase potentials tie at each change of the maximizing pair. No diode conduction, load current, or commutation is being calculated.

Connection to ideal diode rectifiers with an R load

For the balanced phase sources shown here, zero source impedance, ideal uncontrolled diodes and a purely resistive load:

Half-wave, with neutral return (three pulses per source period):

vo=max⁡(vU,vV,vW)

Full-wave bridge (six pulses per source period):

vo=max⁡(vU,vV,vW)−min⁡(vU,vV,vW)

In each R-load case, io=voR. The highest phase forms the three-pulse output; the maximum–minimum envelope displayed here forms the six-pulse output. These are ideal voltage constructions: they do not calculate diode drops, source-inductance overlap, SCR firing or capacitor charging currents. Model consistency checks

The implementation checks the instantaneous phase sum, the loop identity vUV + vVW + vWU = 0, and the opposite-polarity identities. Tiny residuals can come from floating-point arithmetic. These checks do not validate a different physical model.

Keep the conventions straight

Notation and physical meaning

In this lesson, vU, vV, and vW denote instantaneous phase-to-neutral voltages. Vm is their common peak; an “rms” subscript explicitly identifies an RMS value. U, V, W, and N are upright terminal names, not variable indices. The rotating vectors use peak values. The positive phase sequence is U–V–W, corresponding to the original R–S–T names in the source and unchanged technical data keys. The sine function is written sin in all three languages.

The chosen origin is t = 0 at the upward zero crossing of vU, so φU = 0. The cursor is θ = ωt; it is not an adjustable initial phase. Initial phases are defined relative to this same origin and use the sine convention. Te = 1/f; the phase named W is not the period. Changing the origin would be a different operation, which this control does not perform.

θ=ωt,ω=2πf
Peak, instantaneous, and RMS are different

Teaching addition derived from the sinusoidal model. “ph” means phase-to-neutral and “LL” means line-to-line. For a sine wave, its RMS value is its peak divided by √2. Compare RMS with RMS, or peak with peak—not one of each.

Vph,rms=Vm2
VLL,rms=3Vm2
VLL,rms=3Vph,rms
VLL,peak=3Vm

Example: a 230 V phase RMS value gives about 398.372 V line RMS, 325.269 V phase peak, and 563.383 V line peak. “230/400 V” would be a rounded nominal description, not an exact equality used by this model.

Investigate before revealing the explanation

Each investigation has one question to test. Load its starting point, write a prediction, make the proposed change, and compare your observation with your explanation. Opening a solution does not overwrite your notes.

01120° is an angle, not a fixed time
Predict

Start at θ = 90°, f = 50 Hz. Which phase reaches its positive maximum next? Predict how the time to that maximum changes if the frequency is doubled.

Inspect the laboratory

The test change sets f to 100 Hz, keeping θ = 90°. Compare the 120° time interval, then move the cursor to the next positive phase maximum. The waveform window always spans two periods. As you move the cursor, compare the two angle columns in the table: initial phases stay fixed; instantaneous phases advance.

Check your explanation

V peaks next, at 210°. The angular interval stays at 120°, but Δt = 1/(3f) falls from 6.667 ms to 3.333 ms. Doubling the rate of rotation halves the time for the same angular displacement. Neither moving the cursor nor this frequency change alters φU, φV, or φW. At the same θ, all instantaneous sine arguments are unchanged too.

02Does the line peak occur at the phase peak?
Predict

Use Vm = 100 V and the UV measurement. Start at 90°, where U is at its positive peak. Predict vUV there and whether it is already at its own maximum.

Inspect the laboratory

Apply the test change to move to 60°. Compare both phase potentials and their difference at the two angles.

Check your explanation

At 90°, the potentials are 100 V and −50 V, so vUV = 150 V. At 60°, they are approximately 86.603 V and −86.603 V, giving the line peak 173.205 V. The largest difference is reached before the U peak; the phase voltages cannot simultaneously be +100 V and −100 V. Here φUV stays at +30°: the peak occurs at θ = 60° because θ + φUV = 90°.

03Reverse the probes, not the source sequence
Predict

Start with Vm = 100 V, θ = 0°, and the UV measurement. Predict the signed reading after exchanging the probes. Is the phase change 30°, 120°, or 180°?

Inspect the laboratory

The test change selects VU and displays the reversed-polarity line-voltage set. Keep the angle fixed, then examine other cursor positions.

Check your explanation

vUV is approximately +86.603 V and vVU is −86.603 V. Every sample changes sign; the peak and RMS are unchanged. This is a 180° shift of the same pair voltage. The 30° statement compares a line voltage with a phase voltage, so it is a different comparison. The cursor θ did not move. The initial phases of UV and VU differ by 180° modulo 360°.

04Follow all six positive peaks
Predict

Use a 1 V phase peak. Starting at 0°, record the line voltage with the positive peak at each successive 60° position. Predict when the pattern will repeat.

Inspect the laboratory

The test change moves from 0° to 60°. Continue with two +30° steps at a time. Compare the six line peaks with the three phase peaks.

Check your explanation

The order is WV, UV, UW, VW, VU, WU, then WV again at 360°. The line peaks are √3 V, whereas the phase peaks are 1 V. The original three direct line voltages are 120° apart; including their opposite polarities interleaves three more positive peaks.

05At a boundary, two answers can be correct
Predict

Optional extension. Start at 0° with Vm = 100 V and the maximum–minimum envelope visible. Predict which phases will be highest and lowest at 30°. Will one line voltage uniquely define the maximum?

Inspect the laboratory

Apply the test change to 30°. Then inspect 29° and 31° with the numerical angle field. Record the highest/lowest phases and the envelope value.

Check your explanation

At 30°, U and W both equal +50 V, while V equals −100 V. Therefore vUV = vWV = 150 V, the lower limit of the envelope. Just before the boundary, WV maximizes the difference; just after it, UV does. The voltages remain continuous. A voltage tie alone does not calculate a real diode commutation.

Model, limits, and teaching reference

This laboratory calculates the steady periodic voltages of an ideal balanced three-phase sinusoidal source with fixed U–V–W sequence, equal phase peaks, a common frequency, and an ideal neutral reference. Line voltages are calculated by instantaneous subtraction. The source amplitude and frequency are prescribed, not solved from a generator or a network.

Not modeled: unbalance, harmonics, negative-sequence sources, source impedance, loads, phase or neutral currents, power, star/delta load connections, semiconductor states, or commutation transients. The √3 and ±30° relations shown here apply to the stated balanced sinusoidal source and the stated voltage ordering. They must not be applied indiscriminately to other systems. Numerical input limits: phase peak 0 V or 10⁻⁹–600 V; frequency 1–100 Hz; cursor 0–720°. Values smaller than the supported nonzero voltage range are rejected, not rounded silently to zero.

Teaching reference: Recordatorio Red Trifásica. This lesson retains its U–V–W sine convention, terminal ordering, and phase/line relationships. The interactive diagrams are redrawings. No accompanying presentation is needed to use the lesson.
