01 / EXPLORE AND UNDERSTAND

Control the gate charge, not only the logic level

Follow the source and sink paths. Separate gate behavior from the voltage and current in the power circuit.

Voltage control does not mean zero gate current. Source current changes the gate charge at turn-on; sink current removes it at turn-off. Once the voltage settles, the ideal insulated gate requires no DC current.

Source and sink: compare both transitions

Model scope. The linear equivalent capacitance C_eq represents the insulated gate. The two resistances include driver output resistance and the gate path. These are gate-voltage transitions, not predicted drain/collector switching times; Miller charge, parasitic inductance, leakage, and device current are not simulated. The waveform is the exact periodic state of this RC model. [1]

Why use a low output impedance?

In a complementary NPN/PNP totem pole, the NPN sources gate current and the PNP sinks it. A driver must support both directions. A larger turn-on impedance can moderate the edge without forcing the same change at turn-off. A diode-steered path or asymmetric feedback can make the two directions different; the detailed slew rate still depends on the power circuit.

iG = Ceq dvG,rel/dt
τon = RonCeq;  τoff = RoffCeq
Pdrive = fsVdriveCeq(vmax − vmin)

For a real device, use its gate-charge curves and operating conditions. The Miller plateau links gate charge to drain/collector voltage. Do not interpret VGS(th) as full enhancement, or assume the fastest possible turn-off is always safest: loop inductance can convert a rapid current fall into overvoltage.

At the first instant, the initially uncharged C₁ bypasses R₁ and the gate voltage is zero, so R_GS carries zero current. That does not make either resistor irrelevant later. At DC both capacitors carry zero current, but R_GS draws an external divider current. This is not current through the gate oxide. With C₁ disconnected, the initial current is 12/(Rₛ + R₁).

Model scope. Exact linear two-capacitor step response; no Miller effect, driver current limit, or stray inductance. The added capacitor can change the entire transient, not just its first instant.

Trace the optocoupler and two inversion stages

With light present, the phototransistor drives Q₁ on. Q₁ pulls Q₃’s base low, so Q₃ turns off and R₃ pulls the gate high. Without light, Q₁ turns off, Q₃ turns on, and the gate is pulled low. This circuit uses a resistive pull-up and an active pull-down; it is not the complementary totem pole of the previous section.

Signal isolation is not a power supply. The output stage needs auxiliary energy referenced to its local source/emitter. The complete insulation barrier includes the auxiliary supply and layout. No voltage rating, creepage distance, or safety category is certified by this diagram.

Apply enough gate current for long enough

Study pulse duration, repetition, synchronization, isolation, and gate protection as different parts of the same trigger chain.

A short gate pulse may end before latching

The gate initiates conduction. It must provide adequate current while the anode current reaches the latching level I_L. Removing the gate is not a turn-off command for an already latched SCR.

Model scope. An additional constant-voltage RL reference isolates pulse-duration effects: 48 V source, 1.2 V SCR drop, I_L = 50 mA, I_GT = 50 mA, V_GK = 1.5 V, and a 2 ms gate window. The plotted anode current assumes uninterrupted conduction from zero. It is not an actual current trace after failed triggering or during gate gaps. [2]

Read the result without overinterpreting the pulse train
I∞ = (48 − 1.2)/R

A sufficient single pulse in this reference must last at least t_L and have I_G ≥ I_GT. A train offers repeated opportunities and can reduce gate energy relative to a continuous pulse of the same window and amplitude. It does not guarantee latching if each pulse is too short. The reference test does not add separate pulse widths together or assume that anode current survives the gaps. Real operation depends on the load, current continuity, pulse timing, device data, and temperature. If pulse width equals pulse period, there are no gaps: the train is one uninterrupted pulse over the gate window.

With L > 0, I∞ ≤ I_L cannot reach the latching level in finite time in this reference. In the ideal L = 0 case the resistive current is established immediately: equality reaches the mathematical threshold, but provides no design margin. I_L is not the holding current I_H, which matters after latching when gate drive is removed.

Four ways to shape a trigger signal

Short pulse. A brief primary excitation transfers a gate pulse. It can be economical, but the anode current may rise too slowly with an inductive load.

Long pulse. A longer gate excitation offers more time to reach latching. Pulse-shaping capacitance can alter the primary waveform; the primary clamp and reset path must still be analyzed.

Auxiliary-winding pulse train. An extra winding N₃ provides feedback in a pulse-generating circuit. Its polarity, transistor dynamics, and core reset determine the oscillation; it is not an automatic guarantee of a sustained train after the command ends.

Clock + AND gate. The command enables clock pulses; an amplifier drives the pulse-transformer primary. This avoids N₃. Distinguish the base resistor, which limits drive current, from the primary-path resistor, which limits primary/collector current.

Circuit-reading qualification. The pulse families above are functional descriptions. A capacitor connected from a base-drive node to return does not, by itself, form a series differentiator. Likewise, pulse extension and self-oscillation cannot be inferred from the mere presence of C or N₃: the actual connections, feedback polarity, reset path, and input waveform must be checked. No transistor-level simulation of those pulse generators is claimed.

The markers identify timing only, not the output of a controlled bridge. Gate returns of different SCRs cannot simply be joined: their cathodes may be at different potentials. Each floating channel needs an appropriate reference and isolation arrangement.

Protect the gate without defeating the trigger

R_GK and C_GK name the external gate-to-cathode resistor and capacitor. Elsewhere, C_eq denotes the equivalent MOSFET/IGBT gate capacitance. These elements trade noise immunity against available trigger current and pulse shape. Their values must preserve reliable triggering while respecting the gate’s positive/negative voltage, current, and power limits. A shunt capacitor is not a substitute for correct power-terminal dv/dt control.

04 / EXPLORE AND UNDERSTAND

Temperature is a system result

Do not confuse the case temperature with the junction temperature, or a low average loss with immunity to a short pulse.

Protection has several time scales. Cooling limits temperature rise from average loss; snubbers shape fast transients; TVS/MOV devices divert surge current; fuses interrupt sustained fault paths; crowbars force a shutdown path. No one function replaces all the others.

Follow the heat from junction to ambient

Model scope. Steady-state series thermal network for one device at imposed average loss. Temperatures do not feed back into electrical losses. Transient thermal impedance, airflow prediction, heat-sink geometry, and shared heat sinks are outside this model.

Shared initial benchmark: 25 W of loss, 25 °C ambient, and thermal resistances 0.4 / 0.2 / 1.5 K/W from junction to case, case to heat sink, and heat sink to ambient. It gives heat-sink / case / junction temperatures of 62.5 / 67.5 / 77.5 °C. Each laboratory has its own controls; these are initial comparison values, not a live link or a check of transient thermal impedance or safe operating area.

Which part of the thermal path can be improved?

Notation shared with the Power Semiconductors laboratory: θ identifies thermal resistance; j = junction, c = case, h = heat sink, and a = ambient. P is the imposed average semiconductor loss. The CSV retains its existing keys: Rjc → R_θjc, Rcs → R_θch, Rsa → R_θha, and Ts → T_h.

Tj − Ta = P(Rθjc + Rθch + Rθha)
Rθha,max = (Tj,target − Ta)/P − Rθjc − Rθch

Fins, forced air, and the ambient conditions affect the sink-to-ambient path. Mounting and interface material affect the case-to-sink path. Neither directly removes the internal junction-to-case resistance. A negative R_θha,max means that no nonnegative heat-sink resistance meets this target with the other assumptions unchanged. A favorable temperature is not proof that blocking voltage, surge current, SOA, or insulation limits are met.

A temperature difference of 1 K equals a difference of 1 °C. Thermal resistance in K/W converts watts of loss into a temperature difference; it does not represent electrical resistance.

Technical qualification. A TVS is optimized for transient protection, not necessarily for a higher voltage rating than every Zener diode. Distinguish maximum working/standoff voltage, breakdown voltage at its test current, and clamping voltage at the specified surge. For a MOV, α is a local nonlinearity parameter; α = 35 is not a universal boundary between device technologies. Package size, pulse shape, repetition, temperature, and connections determine actual energy and voltage capability.

A MOV can be connected line-to-line, line-to-return, or in another suitable arrangement. “Diverting current” does not necessarily mean sending it to protective earth. The path must be part of the intended circuit and insulation design. A sustained overvoltage can overheat either technology unless another protection limits or interrupts the fault.

Clear the fault before its stress propagates

Distinguish peak let-through current, melting, arcing, total clearing, and the role of the circuit that supplies the fault.

Melting is not the same as complete interruption

Model scope. Prescribed current envelopes, not a fuse-selection simulation. Prospective current is a 50 Hz half-sine. Actual current follows it until the chosen melting time, then falls linearly to zero during the chosen arc time. Both times are inputs, not predictions from a fuse rating. The I²t values are exact integrals of these drawn curves. [5]

Current continues during the arc after the fusible element melts. Include that interval when comparing let-through stress with semiconductor withstand. Current limiting is a property of appropriate fuse operation under specified fault conditions, not a guarantee that every fuse limits every fault peak. A fuse is sacrificial and normally requires replacement; removing future gate commands does not turn off an SCR already conducting.

If the source limits current strongly, the crowbar can remain conducting without clearing the fuse quickly. Turn an observation into an explanation

Eight guided investigations connect gate drive, isolation, pulse shaping, and protection. Change one condition at a time.

Predict → change → observe → explain. Prepare buttons change only the relevant model. Write your own conclusion before opening the explanation. Notes stay in this browser when local storage is available; export a copy before moving or replacing the file.

01Separate turn-on and turn-off

Keep the capacitance and drive voltage fixed. Increase only R_on from 10 to 50 Ω, then restore it and increase only R_off. Predict the sourced/sunk current peaks and the two transition slopes. Inspect whether the gate has enough time to settle at high switching frequency.

Check my reasoning

Increasing one path resistance slows that edge and reduces its initial current. In the periodic model it can also change the voltage reached before the next edge. Driver supply energy is V_drive C_eq (v_max − v_min) per cycle; do not equate it to power-stage switching loss.

02Initial boost versus final voltage

Toggle C₁ while keeping Rₛ, R₁, and R_GS fixed. Then keep C₁ connected and reduce R_GS. Which initial current changes, and which steady-state gate voltage changes? Explain the role of each resistor at 0⁺ and at steady state.

Check my reasoning

An initially uncharged C₁ bypasses R₁ at 0⁺, giving 12/Rₛ into the gate capacitance. R_GS initially has zero voltage, but determines the eventual divider voltage. Connecting C₁ does not change the DC divider. Lower R_GS lowers the final gate voltage and increases external DC current.

03A moving source is a moving reference

Use 12 V of drive and move the source potential from 0 to 300 V. Compare the two references before inspecting V_GS. Then examine the optocoupler truth sequence and identify where gate-charging energy comes from.

Check my reasoning

A source-referenced output follows the source: V_G becomes 312 V and V_GS remains 12 V. A ground-referenced 12 V output would imply −288 V in the imposed-voltage calculation, revealing the wrong reference. The local auxiliary supply, not the light signal itself, supplies the output driver energy.

04Short pulse, long pulse, or train?

Start with L = 100 mH, R = 100 Ω, and I_G = 100 mA. Compare a 50 µs pulse with a long pulse. Next select a train of 50 µs pulses, then increase each pulse to 200 µs. Compare the longest continuous pulse with t_L, not the sum of all pulse widths.

Check my reasoning

For this reference t_L is approximately 113 µs. A single 50 µs pulse is too short; a long pulse passes the reference test. Repeating short pulses is not automatically sufficient. A 200 µs pulse provides enough uninterrupted gate time under these assumptions. In all cases real device and load conditions still matter.

05The heat sink cannot remove every temperature rise

Prepare this investigation: P = 30 W, T_a = 35 °C, R_θjc = 0.5 K/W, R_θch = 0.25 K/W, R_θha = 1.25 K/W, and a junction target of 100 °C. Predict T_j before checking the result. Keep the other settings fixed and reduce R_θha to zero. Then keep R_θha = 0 and increase P to 100 W. Predict whether the target can still be met. Can changing only the external heat sink solve this last case?

Check my reasoning

Initially the total thermal resistance is 2 K/W, so T_j = 35 + 30 × 2 = 95 °C. With R_θha = 0 and P = 30 W, T_j = 57.5 °C. Raising P to 100 W while keeping this ideal heat sink gives T_j = 110 °C, above the 100 °C target. The required R_θha,max is −0.10 K/W: no nonnegative heat-sink resistance can satisfy the target. Reduce loss, ambient temperature, or the remaining thermal resistances. The ideal heat sink removes only the sink-to-ambient temperature rise.

06A large R is not automatically a better snubber

Keep V = 200 V, L_s = 1 µH, C = 20 nF, and I₀ = 5 A. Compare R = 0, 10, and 40 Ω. Track the peak switch voltage, initial Ri step, and ringing. Then restore R = 10 Ω and increase C to 40 nF. Distinguish v_C from v_sw.

Check my reasoning

R = 0 stores and returns energy without resistive damping. Finite R damps the oscillation but also contributes Ri to switch voltage. Increasing C lowers the initial capacitor slope for fixed I₀, changes damping and resonance, and doubles the full-swing C V² f estimate. The best R depends on the circuit and initial conditions.

07The surge source helps set the clamping voltage

Use the unidirectional TVS model at +400 V with V_k = 200 V and r_d = 1 Ω. Change R_s from 10 to 20 Ω. Predict voltage, current, and pulse energy. Reverse the surge to −400 V, then compare a bidirectional TVS. Finally change the MOV exponent without treating it as a technology label.

Check my reasoning

Increasing source resistance reduces surge current and moves the TVS operating point closer to its knee. A unidirectional device conducts forwards under reverse surge polarity; a bidirectional device uses an avalanche branch in either direction. A larger MOV exponent gives a smaller fractional voltage change for a given current ratio, not an automatic energy rating.

08A current-limited supply changes fault clearing

First compare fuse melting and clearing in the fuse plot. Then prepare the crowbar at 48 V, R_s = 1 Ω, pre-arcing I²t = 5 A²s, and 40 A current limit. Reduce the limit to 1 A. Does triggering guarantee disconnection within 10 ms? Explain which approximation becomes weak.

Check my reasoning

With 40 A, the constant-I²t melting estimate is 3.125 ms, before adding detection and arcing. At 1 A it becomes 5 s, far beyond the displayed deadline. That long-time extrapolation is not a fuse prediction: thermal losses and manufacturer time-current curves are needed. The SCR must withstand the fault until the upstream circuit really interrupts it.
