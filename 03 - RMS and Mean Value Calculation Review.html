Calculating RMS and mean value

One waveform, two different questions: what is its net effect, and how much resistive heating does it produce? Predict, explore, and then calculate.

UnderstandExploreCalculatePractice

Two concepts, not two names for the same thing

We use current i(t) in amperes. The same definitions apply to voltage. Positive and negative current refer to a chosen reference direction. All averages below use a complete repeating interval T.

Mean value

Signed area → divide by time

The constant level with the same signed area over T. For current, it gives the net charge transferred per unit time. Positive and negative areas can cancel.

It is the DC component: Iav = Idc (also written IDC). The signed mean may be positive, zero, or negative.

RMS value

Square → average → square root

The magnitude of a DC current producing the same average Joule heating in the same constant resistance. Squaring makes both current directions contribute positively.

It is nonnegative. Here RMS includes both DC and AC components.

Time scale and conventions

Changing T only stretches this ideal waveform in time; its mean and RMS do not change. For DC or a constant limiting case, T is any chosen averaging interval, not a unique fundamental period.

The nonnegative amplitude Ia is the peak of the sine/triangle, the rectified peak, or the pulse magnitude. With triangular ripple, the current ranges from I0 − Ia to I0 + Ia.

The two plots use a shared current scale: if current is in mA, its square is in (mA)². Individual readouts may use another explicit SI prefix. Numerical substitutions use amperes; the work export retains A, A², W, and ms.

The sine function is written sin in all three languages.

Two useful extensions

Piecewise-constant levels

For levels Ik lasting Δtk, use time-weighted contributions. The durations must add up to T. Do not average the RMS of separate intervals.

DC plus a zero-mean AC component

For any periodic waveform with finite RMS, the total mean square equals the square of its mean plus the mean square of its zero-mean AC part, defined as i(t) − ⟨i⟩. Every term uses the same averaging interval. Adding squared RMS values of arbitrary components requires a zero cross term (orthogonality), as for different harmonic orders averaged over a full fundamental period. In rectifiers, the extra subscript o denotes output: Io,dc is the mean of io(t), not necessarily the mean source current.

Measurement note: an AC-coupled RMS reading excludes DC. Also, the mean of the absolute value is not the signed mean and is generally not RMS. Check what a meter actually reports.

Average-responding and true-RMS meters

Specify both response and coupling: an AC-only true-RMS measurement rejects DC; total RMS requires AC+DC. For an ideal DC-coupled, sine-calibrated average-responding measurement, the indication is:

Iind=π22⋅⟨|i|⟩

For a positive sine peak Ia, a full-wave rectified sine gives Ia2, equal to its total RMS. A half-wave rectified sine gives Ia22, instead of its total RMS Ia2: about 29.3 % low. These examples preserve DC at the meter input.

An AC-coupled meter sees a different waveform after DC rejection, so do not transfer these indications to it. “True RMS” alone does not specify AC+DC; bandwidth and crest-factor limits also matter.

Predict → change → observe → explain

Choose a short investigation. Make your prediction before loading the changed case, then compare the results in the explorer.

01

Does rectifying change the heating?

Start with a sine: peak 10 A, period 20 ms. Predict how the mean and RMS change when the negative half is reflected upward, without changing its magnitude.

Reveal the explanation
The mean rises from 0 to 20/π ≈ 6.366 A. RMS stays at 10/√2 ≈ 7.071 A because squaring gives exactly the same waveform. Average power in 2 Ω remains 100 W. The rectified period becomes 10 ms: it now repeats twice as often.
02

Twice the duty cycle: twice everything?

Use 10 A unipolar pulses. Predict what happens to the mean, RMS, and average resistor power when the duty cycle changes from 25% to 50%.

Reveal the explanation
The mean doubles: 2.5 → 5 A. RMS grows by √2: 5 → 7.071 A. Average power doubles: 50 → 100 W in 2 Ω. The squared-current area doubles, so its square root does not double.
03

Can ripple be thermally free?

Start with a DC level of 6 A and zero ripple. Predict what changes when a zero-mean triangular ripple of amplitude 4 A is added. The total current then ranges from 2 to 10 A.

Reveal the explanation
The mean stays at 6 A. RMS increases from 6 to √(36 + 16/3) ≈ 6.429 A. Average power in 2 Ω rises from 72 to 82.667 W. The ripple transfers no net charge over a period, but its square has a positive average.

Model, scope, and references

These are prescribed ideal periodic current waveforms, not a circuit simulator. Transients, switching losses, bandwidth limits, and temperature dependence are not modeled. Resistive-heating examples assume a constant 2 Ω resistor. Mean and RMS metrics use closed-form expressions; plot points only draw those expressions. Isolated ideal switching instants do not affect the integrals.

Optional manufacturer references support the heating interpretation and the distinction between AC-only and total RMS.
